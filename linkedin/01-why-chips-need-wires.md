Every chip in your phone is connected to the outside world by wires thinner than a human hair.

Most people have never heard of it. I spent years around it. 🧵

I'm starting a series: Wire-Bond Defects — from first principles to AI. Part 1: why chips need wires at all.

🔹 A silicon die is useless on its own. Its only doors to the outside world are bond pads — tiny aluminium squares placed 35–100 µm apart. A human hair is ~75 µm thick. You cannot solder to that.

🔹 The package is the chip's house. Four jobs: connect, protect, support, cool.

🔹 Inside most packages, the "connect" job is done by wire bonding: a machine welds a gold or copper wire (often 15–50 µm thick) from each bond pad on the die to a lead on the package. One wire per pin — sometimes several for power.

🔹 Why wires, decades later? Cost and flexibility. Flip-chip (solder bumps, no wires) is faster but industry estimates put it at roughly 1.4–5× the cost. Wire bonding is still estimated to make up the large majority of chip interconnects.

🔹 Every wire has three places to fail:
1️⃣ the ball bond on the die
2️⃣ the loop (the wire itself)
3️⃣ the stitch bond on the lead

🔹 Why it matters: bonding happens late — the silicon is already paid for. Some defects hide under the black mould compound. Some pass every test and fail years later in a car or a satellite.
